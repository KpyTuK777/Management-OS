# 1. Purpose

The Governance Retrospective assesses how the M7.1–M7.5 governance process
performed across definition, evidence production, Architecture Review,
correction, Canonical Review, canonicalization, closure, and successor-stage
opening.

Its purpose is to preserve institutional learning without converting historical
interpretation into normative authority. It evaluates the process and its
records; it does not reopen the canonical substance of M7.1–M7.5.

# 2. Scope and Method

## 2.1 Scope

The retrospective covers:

- M7.1 Governance Structure;
- M7.2 Constitution;
- M7.3 Organizational Model;
- M7.4 Development Lifecycle;
- M7.5 Autonomous Development Protocol;
- the orders, definitions, evidence, reviews, corrections, journal entries,

## 2.2 Method

The assessment used four steps:

1. reconstruct each stage's authority lifecycle from repository records;
2. compare the planned artifact and status sequence with the sequence actually
   recorded;
3. classify observed issues as normative, procedural, tooling or workflow, or
   architectural;
4. derive advisory recommendations that do not modify canonical authority.

Observed repository facts are distinguished from retrospective interpretation.
Recommendations identify possible future governed work, not approved changes.

# 4. Demonstrated Strengths

## 4.1 Authority-state separation

The process consistently distinguished creation, evidence, Architecture Review,
Canonical Review, and canonicalization. Definition commits did not approve
their own outputs, review dispositions did not perform transition, and status
labels were reconciled only through separately attributable changes.

This separation was especially visible in M7.4 and M7.5: both definitions were
substantively accepted, yet Canonical Review stopped when prerequisite evidence
was incomplete. Progress pressure did not override the missing authority record.

## 4.2 Finite correction behavior

M7.1 and M7.2 corrections were finite, attributable, and preserved in dedicated
evidence. Findings were resolved without erasing the initial review state or
allowing correction authorship to become approval.

The later M7.4 and M7.5 governance-record corrections also named a return gate.
Once the missing prerequisites were supplied, Canonical Review resumed against a
fixed subject rather than restarting with an ambiguous candidate.

## 4.3 Canonical dependency discipline

Each new stage opened only after its required predecessor acquired canonical
authority. Later definitions cited the Governance Structure, Constitution,
Organizational Model, and Development Lifecycle as their scopes required.

M7.5 demonstrated downstream composability: autonomous execution semantics were
defined without creating a new organizational function, lifecycle stage, or
authority source.

## 4.4 Artifact-class integrity

Orders scoped work, normative documents owned durable governance meaning,
evidence supported claims, reviews owned dispositions, indexes reported state,
and the journal preserved chronology. The classes remained distinguishable even
when multiple records changed in one transition.

## 4.5 Historical preservation

Correction evidence, adverse or blocking findings, reviewed revisions, review
records, and canonicalization events remained discoverable. The process did not
rewrite an earlier record merely because a later state superseded it.

## 4.6 Boundary restraint

Every stage explicitly excluded later-stage substance. The repository stopped
and resolved the attempted M7.6 naming mismatch against the canonical
Governance Structure rather than silently opening a differently named stage.
The final transition opened the canonical M7.6 Retrospective without defining
retrospective conclusions.

# 5. Friction and Recurring Correction Patterns

## 5.1 Review evidence lagged declared review state

M7.4 and M7.5 each reached an asserted Architecture Approved condition before
the Canonical Review gate could identify a complete repository-visible review
record. In M7.4 the missing Architecture Review artifact was the sole blocker.
In M7.5 both the immutable reviewed revision and Architecture Review artifact
were initially absent.

This was procedural friction, not evidence that the reviewed normative meaning
was defective. It nevertheless prevented legitimate Canonical Review and
required explicit return-gate corrections.

## 5.2 Definition and review commit boundaries were inconsistent

Some stages used a clean definition commit followed by review and transition
commits. M7.5's fixed definition commit also contained the preceding M7.4
canonicalization and opening transition. The reviewed protocol remained exactly
identifiable by commit and blob, but the broader commit responsibility made the
review unit less narrow than the ideal definition-only boundary.

## 5.3 Status reconciliation was broad and manual

Each authority change required coordinated updates across the normative source,
root and child indexes, roadmap, backlog, journal, order status, evidence status,
and review references. The repository achieved consistency, but repeated manual
searches were required to detect stale pre-transition text.

This creates avoidable risk that an index or control-plane record may lag the
authoritative disposition even when the underlying judgment is correct.

## 5.4 Stage terminology drift surfaced at transition time

The first M7.5 canonicalization instruction named M7.6 Governance Operations,
while the canonical Governance Structure and Roadmap reserve M7.6 as
Retrospective. The conflict was caught before mutation and corrected by a new
order aligned with canonical authority.

The stop demonstrated governance strength, but the late discovery shows that
successor-stage identity should be validated before preparing a transition.

## 5.5 Review submission readiness was implicit

The process required exact revisions, evidence, independence, index entries,
and consistent status, but no single submission record or checklist confirmed
all prerequisites before Canonical Review was requested. The same class of
deficiency therefore recurred in adjacent stages.

# 6. Defect Classification

## 6.1 Normative defects

### Observed

- M7.1 required six finite corrections to the candidate Governance Structure
  before review completion.
- M7.2 required five finite constitutional refinements before final
  Architecture Approval.

Those defects were corrected before canonicalization and remain attributable in
their correction evidence. No unresolved normative defect is recorded in any
canonical M7.1–M7.5 source.

M7.3, M7.4, and M7.5 completed substantive review without a blocking normative
correction. The M7.4 and M7.5 Canonical Review blockers concerned authority
evidence and revision identity, not their normative models.

## 6.2 Procedural defects

### Observed

- Canonical Review was requested for M7.4 before its Architecture Review record
  was repository-visible.
- Canonical Review was requested for M7.5 before both an immutable definition
  revision and its Architecture Review record were repository-visible.
- Successor-stage naming was not validated against the canonical reservation
  before the first M7.6 opening instruction.

All were detected before the affected authority transition and resolved without
altering the approved normative substance.

## 6.3 Tooling and workflow deficiencies

### Observed

- No automated or single-command check confirmed that every review prerequisite
  and index entry existed before Canonical Review.
- Status reconciliation depended on manual multi-file search and comparison.
- Definition, review, and transition commit responsibility was not uniformly
  narrow.
- Repeated line-ending warnings added noise to validation output without
  identifying a substantive governance defect.

These are workflow observations. They do not prescribe a tool, repository host,
automation product, or implementation.

## 6.4 Architectural observations

### Observed

- The four-power model remained coherent through every correction and
  transition.
- Artifact-class separation made missing authority records discoverable rather
  than allowing claims or indexes to substitute for reviews.
- The lifecycle's fixed-subject and return-gate semantics handled procedural
  correction without conflating it with normative correction.
- The autonomous protocol successfully inherited constraints without creating
  autonomous governance identity or authority.
- Repository-visible truth operated as a real stop condition when instructions
  conflicted with canonical stage identity.

These observations support the stability of the canonical foundation; they are
not new canonical claims.

# 7. Governance Lessons Learned

1. **Substantive quality does not cure missing authority evidence.** A sound
   definition must still stop when its exact revision, independent review, or
   transition prerequisite is absent.
2. **The review subject should be immutable before review is recorded.** Exact
   commit and content identity materially simplify independence, correction,
   and resumed-review reasoning.
3. **Review and transition need separate records and separate verbs.**
   `Approved`, `Canonical`, and `canonicalized` describe distinct events.
4. **Return gates prevent process ambiguity.** Finite corrections were easier to
   resume when the required gate was explicit.
5. **Indexes are controls, not clerical decoration.** Missing or stale index
   entries can expose incomplete repository truth, even though indexes do not
   create authority.
6. **Successor scope must be checked against canonical reservations before
   transition.** Product or architectural intent cannot silently rename a
   canonical stage.
7. **Automation benefits from narrower authority, not broader inference.** M7.5
   became coherent by making delegation explicit and silence non-authorizing.
8. **Historical friction is useful evidence.** Preserving blocked reviews and
   correction cycles made recurring procedural weaknesses visible.

# 8. Recommendations

All recommendations are advisory. None is approved, self-executing, or an
amendment. Adoption requires a separately authorized future work item and the
applicable review lifecycle.

## R1. Establish a review-submission readiness record

Before Architecture or Canonical Review, record one attributable readiness
check covering exact revision identity, evidence completeness, authority,
independence, required indexes, current status, and requested return gate.

**Expected benefit:** prevents review requests from preceding their repository
prerequisites.

## R2. Prefer narrow commit responsibility

Use distinguishable changes for definition, Architecture Review, Canonical
Review, and canonicalization whenever practical. When a change must combine
responsibilities, identify the exact reviewed content independently.

**Expected benefit:** improves review-unit clarity without making a particular
branching or repository workflow normative.

## R3. Define a transition reconciliation manifest

For each authority transition, identify the expected status surfaces and
artifact references before editing them. Validate that earlier live statuses
are absent afterward while historical occurrences remain preserved.

**Expected benefit:** reduces stale-status risk across indexes and the
architecture control plane.

## R4. Validate successor identity before transition

Before opening a stage, compare its identifier, title, artifact class,
dependencies, and permitted scope with the canonical Governance Structure and
Roadmap.

**Expected benefit:** detects naming or scope conflicts before they become
proposed repository mutations.

## R5. Standardize review-record minimum fields

Use a consistent review record containing the exact reviewed revision, review
authority, independence, scope, dependencies, evidence, findings, advisories,
disposition, return or transition gate, and explicit non-transition boundary.

**Expected benefit:** preserves complete review attribution and simplifies
resumed-gate validation.

## R6. Add implementation-independent consistency checks

Define checkable assertions for active-stage count, active-task count, artifact
discoverability, exact reviewed revision, status agreement, preserved review
history, and absence of unauthorized successor substance.

**Expected benefit:** makes governance conformance observable without granting
the checking mechanism authority.

## R7. Preserve correction-class distinctions

Continue labeling normative, procedural, evidence, tooling or workflow, and
architectural findings separately. Do not modify normative content to cure a
recording defect when the reviewed meaning is unchanged.

**Expected benefit:** keeps correction scope proportional and prevents process
friction from causing unnecessary canonical changes.

# 9. Residual Risks

1. Manual reconciliation can still leave a live status surface stale.
2. A future implementation may confuse protocol condition with lifecycle state
   despite the canonical prohibition.
3. A review may be described as complete before its exact repository record is
   available.
4. Combined change sets may obscure the narrow subject of independent review.
5. Advisory recommendations may be treated as approved work unless their
   non-authoritative status remains explicit.
6. Stage terminology may drift when instructions rely on memory rather than the
   canonical reservation.

These risks are advisory observations. They create no new task, priority,
authority, or prohibition beyond canonical governance.

# 10. Conclusion

M7.1–M7.5 produced a coherent canonical governance foundation. The strongest
demonstrated property was not frictionless progression, but the ability to stop,
record finite defects, preserve history, resume at the correct gate, and keep
judgment separate from transition.

The recurring weakness was sequencing of repository-visible review evidence:
substantively sound artifacts reached Canonical Review before all prerequisite
records or immutable revision identities were present. This did not corrupt the
canonical outputs because the governance system stopped progression and required
correction.

Future governance evolution would benefit from narrower change boundaries,
explicit submission readiness, deterministic transition manifests, canonical
successor validation, and checkable consistency assertions. Those improvements
must be considered through separately authorized governance; this retrospective
does not adopt them.
