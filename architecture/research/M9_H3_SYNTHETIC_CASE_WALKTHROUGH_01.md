## Known limitations

# Context

A logistics operation introduced manager review before safety incidents could
be entered into the formal register. The monthly dashboard subsequently showed
a large decline in recorded incidents. Leadership initially interpreted the
decline as improved safety.

The reporting mechanism, not only the reported result, had changed. Manager
review occurred before formal registration. Supervisors described the review as
an attempt to improve data quality. Workers perceived that reporting could
trigger blame.

Anonymous comments, maintenance notes, and first-aid supply usage suggested
that minor events might be continuing outside the formal register. Some events
were being reclassified as coaching conversations or resolved locally.

Safety, privacy, due process, and anti-retaliation considerations bounded what
could be inspected and how. Reported incidents, actual incidents, near misses,
coaching conversations, local resolution, maintenance activity, first-aid
usage, and anonymous signals did not have interchangeable meanings.

The actual population of incidents and near misses remained unobserved. The
monthly decline described the formal register under a changed reporting path;
it did not directly describe the underlying safety condition.

# Evidence

## Observation

### Reporting-path observations

- Manager review was introduced before entry into the formal incident register.
- The formal dashboard later showed a large decline in reported incidents.
- Some submitted events were reclassified as coaching conversations or handled
  locally.
- Supervisors stated that manager review was intended to improve data quality.
- Workers stated or indicated that reporting was perceived as likely to trigger
  blame.

### Related-signal observations

- Anonymous comments were present.
- Maintenance notes were present.
- First-aid supply usage was present.
- These related signals suggested possible continuing minor events but did not
  identify a complete incident population.

### Evidence-condition observations

- Draft, accepted, rejected, and reclassified report states were not equivalent.
- Dashboard inclusion depended on the changed reporting and classification path.
- Missing detail remained where privacy or non-reporting prevented
  reconstruction.
- Absence from the formal register did not demonstrate absence of an event.

## Interpretation

### Initial interpretation

Leadership interpreted the dashboard decline as improved safety.

### Competing interpretations

- The decline could reflect a real reduction in incidents.
- The decline could reflect fewer submissions reaching the formal register.
- The decline could reflect reclassification into coaching conversations or
  local handling.
- The decline could reflect more than one of these conditions at the same time.
- The relationship among blame perception, reporting behavior, classification,
  and the dashboard decline could not be established causally from the preserved
  material.

### Interpretation boundary

Maintenance notes, first-aid usage, and anonymous comments were consistent with
the possibility that minor events continued, but they did not prove individual
incidents or determine their frequency. The dashboard decline was Evidence of
fewer formally recorded incidents under the changed mechanism, not proof of
improved safety and not proof by itself of reporting suppression.

# Operational Diagnosis

## Matter under review

The apparent reduction in formal incident reporting could not be interpreted
honestly without examining the reporting mechanism that produced the count.

## Formation of understanding

1. The dashboard showed a large decline after manager pre-review began.
2. The initial safety-improvement interpretation relied on the dashboard trend.
3. The reporting path revealed that manager review occurred before formal
   registration.
4. Draft, rejected, reclassified, accepted, and locally handled events had
   different routes to dashboard inclusion.
5. Worker accounts introduced a possible reporting disincentive associated with
   blame.
6. Supervisor accounts preserved a different stated intent: improved data
   quality.
7. Maintenance, first-aid, and anonymous signals raised doubt about whether the
   dashboard decline represented the underlying event population.
8. Missing reports and privacy-bounded detail prevented full reconstruction.

## Working diagnostic statement

The observed decline in formally reported incidents was materially affected by
a changed observation and classification mechanism. The preserved information
supported concern that reporting suppression could be contributing to the
decline, while leaving the extent of suppression and the underlying safety trend
unknown.

## Unresolved uncertainty

- Whether the actual incident rate decreased, increased, or remained stable.
- How many events were never submitted.
- How many local or coaching classifications corresponded to reportable events.
- Whether blame perception changed reporting behavior and by how much.
- Whether improved data-quality review contributed to any legitimate
  classification change.
- How proxy-signal changes related to actual incidents.
- Whether other operational factors affected the observed period.

No scoped Cause was accepted as complete. The diagnosis remained sufficient to
challenge the initial dashboard interpretation without claiming full causal
reconstruction.

# Human Judgment

## Point at which human judgment begins

Human judgment begins when the preserved relationships must be interpreted for
safety significance and action under incomplete Evidence. The records establish
that the reporting path and dashboard population changed. They do not decide:

- whether a specific reclassified event should count as an incident;
- whether worker accounts, supervisor intent, and proxy signals justify a safety
  intervention;
- what privacy-respecting inquiry is proportionate;
- what level of uncertainty is acceptable for action;
- whether direct reporting should be restored;
- how manager review should be positioned;
- what escalation protection is required; or
- how safety, due process, and anti-retaliation obligations should be balanced.

## AI assistance used

AI assists by surfacing:

- the timing relationship between manager pre-review and the dashboard decline;
- the distinction among submission, review, registration, classification, and
  dashboard inclusion;
- changed metric semantics;
- the difference between absence of reports and evidence of absence;
- provenance and disagreement across worker and supervisor accounts;
- missingness and possible selection effects;
- alternative interpretations; and
- where proxy signals were at risk of being treated as confirmed incidents.

## Decisions AI does not make

AI does not:

- classify an event as a safety incident;
- determine whether reporting suppression occurred as fact;
- infer misconduct or blame;
- exercise safety, personnel, privacy, or access authority;
- accept a Cause;
- choose the intervention;
- authorize direct reporting, manager review, or escalation;
- determine whether the historical Decision was correct; or
- adopt Learning for an organization.

# Decision

The modeled accountable safety authority decided to:

- restore direct reporting;
- preserve manager review as a subsequent classification step;
- prohibit deletion of the original submission; and
- create a protected escalation route.

The Decision was made by the modeled human safety authority. Management OS
preserved the Evidence basis, uncertainty, accounts, and reporting-path
relationships; it did not exercise the authority.

# Change

The actual modeled Change:

- separated submission from manager review;
- separated review from classification;
- separated classification from dashboard inclusion;
- restored direct reporting;
- retained original report identity;
- made later classification traceable without erasing prior state;
- bounded access to preserved reports; and
- provided a protected escalation route.

Adoption was incomplete. One team continued informal handling. The actual
Change therefore differed from complete uniform adoption of the Decision.

# Outcome

After direct reporting returned:

- formally reported incidents increased;
- more near-miss detail became visible;
- incomplete adoption remained because one team continued informal handling;
  and
- no reliable basis emerged for concluding that the underlying incident rate
  increased or decreased.

The earlier apparent improvement was reinterpreted as potentially affected by
reporting suppression. The magnitude of suppression remained unknown.

The observed increase in reports was a reporting Outcome. It was not Evidence
by itself of a worse underlying safety Outcome, and it did not establish that
the historical Change caused any change in actual incident frequency.

# Learning

The completed historical branch produced the following bounded Learning:

- A decline in a safety dashboard could not be interpreted without the rules
  and path by which events became dashboard entries.
- Reported incidents, actual incidents, near misses, coaching conversations,
  local handling, and proxy signals required separate meanings.
- Original submissions and later classification needed to remain linked without
  historical deletion.
- The initial positive interpretation was weakened when the changed reporting
  mechanism and non-register signals became visible.
- Worker perception and supervisor intent could coexist without either account
  resolving actual reporting behavior.
- The Outcome of restored reporting was increased report visibility, not proof
  of an underlying safety-rate direction.
- Missing-not-at-random information limited reconstruction and causal
  attribution.
- The historical Decision and actual Change were distinguishable because
  adoption remained incomplete.

This Learning remained attached to the synthetic completed case. It did not
become organizational Learning or validate H3.

## Replayed Decision reasoning

### Decision question restated

What bounded human action could restore observability and traceability without
claiming that the underlying incident rate or the magnitude of suppression was
known?

### Evidence basis revisited

- The dashboard decline followed a change in the reporting mechanism.
- Pre-registration manager review affected the population entering the formal
  register.
- Worker accounts indicated possible blame-related reporting disincentive.
- Supervisor accounts stated a data-quality purpose.
- Proxy signals supported further concern but did not establish incident count.
- Missingness prevented reconstruction of the unreported population.

### Assumptions made explicit

- Direct reporting could preserve original submissions before later
  classification.
- Subsequent manager review could remain compatible with direct submission.
- Preserving original state could make classification correction traceable.
- A protected route could make escalation available without proving how it would
  affect reporting behavior.
- Incomplete adoption remained possible.

### Alternatives visible in the reasoning branch

- Retain pre-registration manager review unchanged while continuing observation.
- Remove manager review entirely.
- Restore direct submission while retaining later review and classification.
- Change dashboard interpretation without changing the reporting path.

No alternative was enacted in Replay. No modeled alternative Outcome was
generated.

### Authority check

The accountable safety authority retained the Decision. Classification,
privacy, personnel, access, and operational responsibilities remained with
their human owners. AI surfaced tradeoffs and uncertainty but did not select an
alternative.

### Replayed rationale attached to the historical Decision

The historical Decision was reconsidered as an observability-and-traceability
intervention under unresolved safety-rate uncertainty. Its immediate purpose
was not recorded as reducing incidents. It restored direct submission,
preserved subsequent review, prevented deletion of original state, and created
a protected escalation route while retaining that adoption and safety effects
were unknown.

## Historical branch preserved

Replay did not change the historical Decision. The same modeled accountable
safety authority still decided to restore direct reporting, retain manager
review after submission, prohibit deletion of the original submission, and
create a protected escalation route.

Replay did not change the actual Change or Outcome. Reported incidents still
rose, near-miss detail still increased, one team still continued informal
handling, and the underlying incident-rate direction remained unknown.

# Additional Learning

The Replay branch made the following additional understanding visible without
rewriting Historical Learning:

- The historical Decision could be understood as changing the observation
  mechanism and correction trace, not as directly changing the underlying
  incident rate.
- Restoring direct reporting and retaining subsequent manager review addressed
  different responsibilities; treating them as mutually exclusive had hidden a
  useful semantic distinction.
- Preventing deletion of original submissions protected later reconstruction,
  while later classification still required separate accountable judgment.
- The protected escalation route concerned access to voice and authority, but
  the preserved Outcome did not show whether worker perception changed.
- Incomplete adoption was not only an implementation detail; it limited what
  the reporting Outcome could mean across the operation.
- An alternative reasoning branch could examine other options without needing
  to invent their Changes or Outcomes.
- The Decision rationale became more bounded when “improve safety” was separated
  from “restore observability and traceability under uncertainty.”
- The same historical Outcome supported reflection on Decision scope even
  though it could not establish Decision correctness or causal effectiveness.

The additional Learning did not establish that the historical Decision was
right or that any alternative Decision would have been better.

# H3 Reflection

## Result

**Partially**

## Reflection record

Replay produced additional bounded Learning beyond the first completed pass. It
made the Decision's observability-and-traceability scope more explicit,
separated direct submission from later classification, exposed incomplete
adoption as a limit on Outcome meaning, and allowed alternative reasoning to be
examined without creating alternative Outcomes.

The result is only partial because the first pass already preserved the central
measurement-system Learning, and the Replay used an authored synthetic history
rather than a completed real operational experience. The walkthrough cannot
show that a real manager obtained substantially more Learning than from the
original experience, that the additional understanding would persist, or that
Management OS caused it.

This `Partially` result describes the internal training walkthrough only. It
neither confirms nor falsifies H3.

# Observed Architectural Friction

- The preserved material did not show a precise sequence among every draft,
  rejection, reclassification, local resolution, and dashboard entry, limiting
  temporal reconstruction.
- Worker perception and supervisor intent were both preserved, but the case did
  not expose how either account related to individual reporting actions.
- Maintenance notes, first-aid usage, and anonymous comments were difficult to
  relate to the matter without risking conversion of proxy signals into incident
  Evidence.
- The boundary between a reportable event, a near miss, a coaching conversation,
  and local handling remained semantically ambiguous in the preserved material.
- Missing-not-at-random information made the difference between known absence,
  unknown absence, and non-reporting hard to express compactly.
- The Decision record and actual Change required a visible relationship to the
  one team's continued informal handling; otherwise Change fidelity appeared
  more complete than observed.
- Replay naturally required explicit links from each reasoning statement to the
  information available at the Replay point.
- The Replay branch needed persistent separation from the historical branch at
  Interpretation, rationale, and alternative levels while sharing the same
  Observation, Evidence, Decision, Change, and Outcome.
- It was difficult to express additional Learning without making it appear to
  replace or improve the Historical Learning.
- The Outcome contained a clearer reporting effect than safety effect, creating
  semantic tension whenever a single Outcome label was used.
- Authority over safety classification, report access, personnel implications,
  and Learning acceptance remained distributed and could not be represented as
  one generic owner.

These are observations from the walkthrough. They are not recommendations,
architecture changes, new objects, or new capabilities.
