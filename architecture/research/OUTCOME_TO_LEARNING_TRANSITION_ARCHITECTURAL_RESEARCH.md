# Outcome-to-Learning Transition — Architectural Research

## Research Status

```text
Research evidence only
Implementation-independent, non-authoritative, and non-normative
No Outcome object, Learning object, success model, evaluation system, lifecycle, governance mechanism, validation, milestone, backlog, or work created
```

## 1. Purpose

This paper investigates the architectural relationship from observed and
verified Outcome to bounded, correctable Learning in Management OS. It examines
what each meaning owns, which Evidence supports observation and interpretation,
how attribution remains bounded, how Learning may follow different Outcome
conditions, and whether existing canonical semantic kinds appear conceptually
sufficient.

The research does not define a new Outcome or Learning object, success model,
performance framework, evaluation system, workflow, lifecycle, governance
mechanism, validation method, or implementation.

## 3. Preserved Semantic Boundaries

```text
Outcome Observation ≠ Outcome Interpretation
Outcome Interpretation ≠ Outcome Attribution
Outcome Attribution ≠ Learning
Positive Outcome ≠ Correct Decision
Negative Outcome ≠ Bad Decision
Outcome ≠ Causal proof
Learning ≠ Success declaration
Learning ≠ Canonical truth
Learning ≠ Future authorization
```

Additional boundaries:

- Actual Change is not Outcome.
- Execution completion is not Outcome success.
- A modeled or predicted result is not observed Outcome.
- Recommendation acceptance is not Outcome.
- Human confidence or satisfaction is not sufficient Outcome Evidence.
- A favorable Outcome cannot rewrite prior Evidence or erase uncertainty.
- An unfavorable Outcome cannot retroactively make a Decision unauthorized.
- Learning cannot justify harm merely because something was learned.
- AI cannot verify Outcome or promote its explanation into Learning authority
  without source-owned Evidence and accountable human judgment.

## 4. Research Meaning of the Transition

The Outcome-to-Learning transition is the relationship between attributable
evidence of operational consequence and a bounded human-reviewable understanding
of what that experience may inform later.

It is not automatic. Outcome does not emit Learning merely because an
observation exists, and Learning does not become valid because a narrative is
stored.

A research-form question is:

> Does attributable Outcome Evidence, interpreted within Decision intent,
> Actual Change, context, comparison, uncertainty, and alternative explanations,
> support a bounded and correctable learning claim whose later use preserves
> provenance and human authority?

The question creates no validation criterion or readiness class.

## 5. Four Distinct Propositions

### 5.1 Outcome Observation

Outcome Observation concerns what operational condition was perceived or
measured after the relevant Actual Change or no-Change condition.

It may preserve:

- observed source and provenance;
- operational subject and scope;
- time and observation horizon;
- condition before and after Change;
- expected and unexpected effects;
- adverse, delayed, displaced, null, and missing observations;
- comparison or baseline context;
- uncertainty, limits, and correction; and
- identity of the observing source or human.

Observation answers:

> What operational condition is attributable as observed?

It does not answer why the condition occurred, whether it satisfies Decision
intent, or what should be learned.

### 5.2 Outcome Interpretation

Outcome Interpretation relates the observed condition to:

- Decision intent and intended condition;
- Actual Change and handoff fidelity;
- baseline, comparison, or expected range;
- timing and duration;
- operational context;
- external factors and concurrent Changes;
- prior Evidence and hypotheses;
- adverse or balancing effects; and
- material uncertainty.

Interpretation may describe the Outcome as intended, unintended, partial, null,
mixed, adverse, delayed, temporary, displaced, or indeterminate for a bounded
claim. These are research descriptions, not canonical states.

Interpretation answers:

> What may the observed condition mean relative to the bounded Decision and
> intended operational condition?

It remains challengeable and cannot become Fact through confidence or reuse.

### 5.3 Outcome Attribution

Outcome Attribution assesses the bounded relationship among:

- human Decision;
- Decision-to-Change handoff fidelity;
- Actual Change;
- observed operational condition;
- plausible mechanism;
- comparison;
- context and external factors;
- alternative explanations; and
- uncertainty.

Attribution answers:

> What, if anything, can be supported about the contribution of the Decision and
> Actual Change to the observed Outcome?

Possible research conclusions include association, plausible contribution,
material contribution, insufficient attribution, or indeterminate. They are not
new canonical attribution kinds.

Outcome occurrence does not establish unique cause. Strong handoff fidelity does
not guarantee contribution. Poor fidelity does not rule out coincidental or
independent favorable Outcome.

### 5.4 Learning

Learning is a bounded, correctable relation from attributable experience to what
may inform later understanding, Decision, Change, or inquiry.

Learning may preserve:

- what was expected;
- what was actually decided and changed;
- what was observed;
- which interpretations were supported or weakened;
- what remains unknown;
- where handoff fidelity changed the intervention;
- which external factors mattered;
- which adverse or balancing effects appeared;
- context and transfer limits;
- correction and later contrary Evidence; and
- conditions under which reuse would require new human judgment.

Learning answers:

> What bounded, evidence-relative understanding from this experience may be
> considered later without becoming authority or rewriting the past?

## 6. Relationship Among the Propositions

```text
Outcome Observation
  → Outcome Interpretation
  → bounded Outcome Attribution, where Evidence permits
  → Learning proposition
  → possible later human use
```

This is explanatory, not a workflow or lifecycle. Any arrow may stop,
remain indeterminate, return for Investigation, or be revised by later Evidence.

Learning does not require strong causal attribution in every case. A person may
learn that Evidence was insufficient, handoff fidelity was unknown, a comparison
was unusable, or no supported conclusion can be made. The learning claim must
match the available Evidence.

## 7. What It Means to Observe Outcome

### 7.1 Operational condition rather than activity

Outcome Observation concerns the condition the Decision intended to affect, not
only the activity through which Change occurred.

Examples of insufficient substitutes include:

- Change marked complete;
- tasks closed;
- users active;
- Recommendation accepted;
- AI interactions increased;
- records created;
- participants satisfied;
- model predicted improvement; or
- Decision owner believed success occurred.

These may be contextual Evidence but cannot alone establish operational
consequence.

### 7.2 Decision-relative verification

An observed condition becomes a verified Outcome only through accountable human
judgment relative to:

- Decision intent;
- intended condition;
- actual Change;
- observation scope and horizon;
- relevant comparison;
- contrary and adverse Evidence; and
- uncertainty and limitations.

The verifier may differ from the Decision owner and Change owner. Verification
authority must remain attributable.

### 7.3 Observation can exist without Outcome conclusion

Source-owned observations may exist while:

- intended condition is unclear;
- Actual Change is unknown;
- comparison is unusable;
- horizon is premature;
- confounders dominate;
- Evidence conflicts; or
- verification owner is absent.

In such cases, Observation should remain Observation and the Outcome claim may
remain indeterminate.

### 7.4 No observation is not negative Outcome

Missing, inaccessible, stale, or semantically invalid Outcome Evidence produces
a limitation. It does not prove that the condition failed to improve.

## 8. Evidence Relationship

### 8.1 Evidence that supports Outcome Observation

Potential Evidence includes:

- source-owned observation of the operational condition;
- source identity, time, scope, and provenance;
- before/after condition or relevant trajectory;
- observation horizon and duration;
- material exposure or denominator;
- adverse, delayed, displaced, and null observations;
- correction history and measure changes;
- missing and contrary Evidence; and
- distinction from Actual Change records.

Evidence of execution is not sufficient unless execution itself is the intended
operational condition, and even then the claim must remain bounded.

### 8.2 Evidence that supports Outcome Interpretation

Interpretation may rely on:

- fixed Decision intent and intended condition;
- Decision rationale and Evidence available at the time;
- Decision-to-Change handoff Evidence;
- source-owned Actual Change Evidence;
- baseline, trajectory, comparable prior case, contemporaneous comparison, or
  intended range;
- context at Decision, execution, and observation time;
- external events and concurrent Changes;
- affected participant Evidence;
- balancing and adverse conditions; and
- known measurement, selection, novelty, and confirmation risks.

Interpretation must cite the Evidence it relates and expose alternatives.

### 8.3 Evidence that supports Attribution

Attribution requires a traceable argument:

```text
Decision intent
  → source-owned Actual Change
  → observed operational difference
  → plausible mechanism
  → credible comparison
  → context and alternative explanations
  → bounded human contribution judgment
```

Missing fidelity Evidence, unknown Actual Change, dominant external factors, or
unusable comparison may leave attribution insufficient even when Outcome is
observed.

### 8.4 Evidence that supports Learning

Learning should be grounded in:

- preserved Decision and Change history;
- Outcome Observation and verification;
- Interpretation and alternative explanations;
- Attribution strength and limits;
- contrary, negative, null, adverse, and indeterminate material;
- context and scope;
- explicit uncertainty;
- correction history; and
- later Evidence that may confirm, qualify, reject, or revise the learning.

A lesson statement without this lineage is not sufficient evidence of valid
Learning.

### 8.5 Evidence non-substitution

| Evidence proposition | Does not establish |
| --- | --- |
| Actual Change occurred | Outcome occurred |
| Outcome was observed | Outcome satisfied Decision intent |
| Intended Outcome was achieved | Decision was correct in every respect |
| Outcome was unfavorable | Decision was bad or unauthorized |
| Interpretation is coherent | Attribution is supported |
| Contribution is supported | Unique causal proof |
| Learning was recorded | Learning is valid, generalizable, or authoritative |
| Later behavior changed | Learning caused improved Outcome |

## 9. Where Observation Ends and Learning Begins

Observation ends at the attributable account of the operational condition.

Learning begins when a human-reviewed proposition relates the experience to
what may inform later understanding or action. This requires Interpretation and
explicit limits.

### Observation-language examples

- “The source recorded the condition within range for four weeks.”
- “The same deviation occurred twice after the Change.”
- “No attributable post-Change evidence exists for the affected source.”
- “The observed burden moved from one team to another.”

### Interpretation-language examples

- “The observed condition is consistent with the intended Outcome, within the
  four-week horizon.”
- “Recurrence weakens the proposition that the Change produced durable
  stabilization.”
- “Missing source Evidence makes the Outcome indeterminate.”
- “The apparent improvement may reflect displaced burden.”

### Learning-language examples

- “Future comparable Decisions should not assume durability from short-horizon
  stabilization Evidence.”
- “The previous comparison did not expose displaced burden and should remain a
  limitation in later reasoning.”
- “No supported causal lesson can be transferred from this case.”

These examples describe language boundaries, not required templates or semantic
objects.

## 10. Boundary Conditions

### 10.1 Positive Outcome is not correct Decision

A positive Outcome may coexist with:

- weak Evidence;
- flawed Interpretation;
- poor handoff fidelity;
- unauthorized Change;
- external favorable events;
- unacceptable risk or harm;
- lucky timing; or
- a Decision process that would be unreliable in repetition.

Positive consequence is material Evidence. It cannot retroactively certify all
prior judgment.

### 10.2 Negative Outcome is not bad Decision

A negative or undesired Outcome may coexist with:

- reasonable judgment under uncertainty;
- sufficient Evidence at Decision time;
- faithful and authorized Change;
- unavoidable external events;
- known accepted risk;
- protective action that prevented worse consequence; or
- an Outcome horizon too narrow to show benefit.

Evaluating Decision quality requires Decision-time Evidence, reasoning, authority,
alternatives, and uncertainty—not Outcome alone.

### 10.3 Outcome is not causal proof

Temporal sequence, handoff fidelity, observed improvement, and human belief do
not establish unique causality. Contribution remains bounded by comparison,
mechanism, external factors, and uncertainty.

### 10.4 Learning is not success declaration

Learning may arise from intended, unintended, mixed, null, adverse, or
indeterminate experience. Its existence does not make the original Decision or
Change successful.

### 10.5 Learning is not failure declaration

Learning after an undesired Outcome does not automatically classify the Decision
or Change as failure. It records what the Evidence may inform later.

### 10.6 Learning is not authorization

A bounded lesson cannot authorize a future Decision or Change. Later context and
Evidence require new accountable human judgment.

## 11. Learning After Different Outcome Conditions

The following conditions are research descriptions, not canonical Outcome
states or success/failure classifications.

### 11.1 Desired Outcome achieved

When the intended operational condition is observed and verified, possible
Learning may concern:

- which Decision assumptions remained valid;
- whether Actual Change preserved Decision meaning;
- which context supported the consequence;
- what external factors also contributed;
- which adverse or balancing effects appeared;
- whether the result persisted;
- what remains unproven; and
- which later contexts are meaningfully comparable.

Prohibited automatic conclusions:

- the Decision was correct in all respects;
- the Change uniquely caused the Outcome;
- the same Change should be repeated;
- the result generalizes;
- risk was justified; or
- implementation or policy should be authorized.

### 11.2 Undesired Outcome

When an adverse or contrary condition is observed, possible Learning may concern:

- incomplete or unfaithful Actual Change;
- changed context;
- missing or contrary Evidence;
- unsupported Interpretation;
- external factors;
- adverse side effects;
- inadequate comparison or horizon;
- Decision conditions that should be reconsidered later; and
- which conclusions cannot be supported.

Prohibited automatic conclusions:

- the Decision was bad;
- the human was at fault;
- Governance failed;
- one Root Cause was found;
- the opposite Change is correct; or
- harm is acceptable because Learning occurred.

### 11.3 Mixed Outcome

A mixed Outcome may contain improvement in one dimension and degradation,
burden, delay, instability, or uncertainty in another.

Possible Learning may concern:

- tradeoffs and balancing conditions;
- scope displacement;
- uneven effects across participants or sources;
- short-term versus durable consequence;
- intended versus unintended dimensions;
- need for more bounded Decision framing; and
- limits of a single success metric.

Mixed evidence should not be collapsed into a binary success or failure label.

### 11.4 Indeterminate Outcome

Outcome may remain indeterminate because:

- intended condition was not fixed;
- Actual Change is unknown;
- source Observation is missing or invalid;
- horizon is premature or inconsistent;
- comparison is unusable;
- context changed materially;
- external factors dominate;
- evidence conflicts; or
- verification ownership is absent.

Learning may still concern:

- which Evidence was missing;
- why observation could not support a conclusion;
- where traceability failed;
- which assumptions were not testable;
- how context or authority limited inquiry; and
- what should not be claimed later.

The correct learning may be that no Outcome or causal learning is currently
supportable.

### 11.5 Null Outcome

A null Outcome—no material observed difference relative to the bounded
comparison—may support Learning about:

- weak or absent intervention effect;
- insufficient duration or intensity;
- wrong mechanism;
- natural variability;
- context mismatch;
- incomplete Change;
- insensitive observation; or
- the possibility that the intended condition was unaffected.

Null is not automatically failure and should not be hidden or reclassified as
insufficient engagement without Evidence.

### 11.6 Unexpected beneficial Outcome

An unanticipated favorable effect may become Evidence for Investigation. It does
not prove the Change should be generalized or that the original Decision intended
the benefit.

### 11.7 Unexpected harmful Outcome

An unanticipated adverse effect may require human attention under existing
authority. Learning should preserve harm, context, Decision and Change history,
and uncertainty without using the lesson to excuse consequence.

## 12. External and Context Factors

### 12.1 Context change

Context may differ across Decision, Change, Outcome Observation, and later reuse.
Relevant changes include:

- demand;
- staffing and capacity;
- policy or authority;
- source state;
- participants;
- dependencies;
- timing;
- market or environment;
- measurement definition; and
- concurrent Decisions and Changes.

Context change may create, hide, reverse, delay, or displace Outcome. Learning
must preserve which context was observed rather than treat the lesson as
context-free.

### 12.2 Unexpected events

Unexpected events may dominate or interact with the Change. They should remain
Evidence and alternative explanation, not be excluded because they complicate
the narrative.

### 12.3 Incomplete Change

Outcome Interpretation should use Evidence of Actual Change, including partial,
substituted, interrupted, reversed, or additional intervention. Learning based
on intended Change when Actual Change differed is unreliable.

### 12.4 Attribution uncertainty

Attribution may remain weak even with strong Outcome Evidence. Learning should
match that strength:

- observed association may support noticing a pattern;
- plausible contribution may support a bounded hypothesis;
- material contribution may support stronger but still contextual learning;
- insufficient attribution should prevent causal learning; and
- indeterminate evidence should preserve the question.

These are research descriptions, not canonical attribution classes.

### 12.5 Handoff fidelity

Poor or unknown Decision-to-Change fidelity changes what can be learned. A later
Outcome may relate to Actual Change but not to the original Decision intent.

Strong fidelity narrows one uncertainty but does not establish causality.

## 13. Bias and Learning Risks

### 13.1 Outcome bias

Judging Decision quality mainly from the result ignores what was knowable at
Decision time and the role of uncertainty.

### 13.2 Hindsight bias

After Outcome, people may treat the observed path as predictable and rewrite
prior uncertainty.

### 13.3 Confirmation bias

Evidence may be selected to support the preferred Decision or Change narrative.

### 13.4 Survivorship bias

Only completed, favorable, or well-documented Changes may produce Learning,
while interrupted, abandoned, adverse, and indeterminate cases disappear.

### 13.5 Narrative closure

A coherent story may collapse Observation, Interpretation, attribution, and
Learning into one causal explanation.

### 13.6 Success contamination

The organizational desire to celebrate may suppress adverse effects,
uncertainty, or external contribution.

### 13.7 Failure contamination

The desire to assign blame may convert an undesired Outcome into a claim about
human fault or one Root Cause.

### 13.8 AI-generated certainty

AI may produce a fluent post hoc explanation that appears more authoritative
than the Evidence. It cannot own attribution or Learning.

## 14. Human Authority

### 14.1 Outcome verification owner

An accountable human owns the judgment that observed Evidence does or does not
support the bounded Outcome claim. The verifier should preserve:

- Decision intent;
- Actual Change;
- Observation source and scope;
- comparison;
- contrary and adverse Evidence;
- uncertainty; and
- limitations.

The verification owner may differ from Decision and Change owners.

### 14.2 Learning judgment

Humans determine what bounded learning is justified and whether it is relevant
to a later situation. The system may preserve and explain Evidence but cannot
promote a lesson into authority.

### 14.3 AI non-authority

AI may help:

- organize Outcome Evidence;
- compare observed condition with stated intent;
- identify missing, contrary, or adverse material;
- surface alternative explanations;
- distinguish Observation from Interpretation language;
- summarize attribution limits; and
- draft candidate learning statements for human review.

AI may not:

- verify its own effect;
- declare Outcome;
- infer unobserved consequence;
- decide attribution;
- certify Decision correctness;
- declare success or failure;
- make Learning authoritative; or
- apply a lesson automatically to future Change.

## 15. Failure Understanding Without New Objects

### 15.1 Desired Outcome not achieved

Existing Outcome Evidence, Interpretation, limitations, attribution uncertainty,
Decision and Change history, and Learning can describe the condition without a
Failure object.

### 15.2 Outcome achieved but learning unsupported

A favorable Outcome may be observed while comparison, attribution, context, or
lineage is insufficient for transferable Learning. Observation should remain
valid without forcing a lesson.

### 15.3 Learning later contradicted

Existing correction history, later Evidence, new Interpretation, and bounded
human judgment can revise Learning without rewriting the original Outcome.

### 15.4 No Outcome Evidence

Missing Outcome Evidence is a limitation or indeterminate claim, not negative
Outcome and not automatic failure.

### 15.5 Harm with useful Learning

An adverse Outcome may produce useful Learning. The Learning does not transform
harm into success or justify repeating the Change.

## 16. Product Implications

### 16.1 Distinctions the product may need to preserve

Without proposing objects or implementation, the research suggests future
product evidence should keep visible:

- Decision intent;
- Actual Change and handoff fidelity;
- Outcome Observation source, scope, horizon, and correction;
- verification owner;
- Interpretation and alternative explanations;
- attribution strength and uncertainty;
- adverse, balancing, delayed, displaced, null, and missing Evidence;
- learning statement, context, limits, and correction history;
- later use, rejection, or revision; and
- distinction between Learning and authority.

### 16.2 Questions the product may help humans ask

- What condition was actually observed?
- Which source owns the Observation?
- What was the intended condition and horizon?
- What Actual Change occurred?
- Was handoff fidelity known?
- Which context or external factors changed?
- What comparison is credible?
- What Interpretation is supported, and what alternatives remain?
- What attribution can be claimed?
- What remains indeterminate?
- What Learning is justified?
- Which future contexts are materially comparable?
- What should not be reused or authorized?

These are research implications, not UI, workflow, schema, or product
requirements.

## 17. Existing Capability Sufficiency

### 17.1 Relevant existing responsibilities

The canonical architecture already contains conceptual responsibilities for:

- Operational Matter and continuity;
- bounded Investigation;
- source-owned Observation and Evidence;
- provenance, uncertainty, contradiction, correction, and traceability;
- Interpretation, hypotheses, scoped Cause, and modeled alternatives;
- Recommendation and human Decision separation;
- source-owned action and Actual Change continuity;
- Outcome observation and verification;
- Learning, Memory, and Knowledge with bounded authority; and
- external Governance for any actual review, transition, or authority change.

### 17.2 Conceptual sufficiency finding

Existing semantic kinds appear sufficient to represent:

```text
Outcome Observation
  → human-reviewed Interpretation
  → bounded Attribution, where supported
  → contextual and correctable Learning
  → possible later human consideration
```

No evidence in the current research requires a new Outcome object, Learning
object, success object, transition capability, performance framework, lifecycle,
or governance mechanism.

### 17.3 Why existing kinds appear sufficient

- Observation owns source-attributable condition.
- Evidence owns support and contradiction for bounded claims.
- Interpretation owns explanatory meaning without Fact promotion.
- human verification owns Outcome judgment.
- attribution can remain a bounded Interpretation supported by Evidence.
- Learning owns contextual reuse without authority.
- Memory and Knowledge preserve lineage and correction within their existing
  boundaries.

### 17.4 What remains unproven

Conceptual sufficiency does not demonstrate that current product realization can:

- retrieve complete Outcome Evidence across source systems;
- distinguish activity from consequence reliably;
- preserve measurement meaning and correction;
- connect Outcome with Actual Change and Decision intent;
- expose adverse, delayed, displaced, and null effects;
- support credible comparison and attribution;
- prevent outcome and hindsight bias;
- keep AI-generated explanations non-authoritative;
- preserve learning context and transfer limits; or
- show that Learning improves later Decision or Outcome.

These are product, evidence, and validation questions.

## 18. Open Research Questions

1. What Evidence is sufficient to establish Outcome across different
   source-owned operational conditions?
2. How can verification ownership remain explicit when Decision, Change, and
   Outcome sources differ?
3. What horizons distinguish temporary from sustained consequence?
4. How should mixed and displaced Outcomes remain visible without a success
   score?
5. Which comparisons are credible in recurrent operational situations?
6. How can attribution remain useful without one Root Cause or causal overclaim?
7. When is a learning statement sufficiently grounded for later consideration?
8. How should later contradictory Evidence revise Learning without rewriting the
   original Outcome?
9. Can current Memory and Knowledge responsibilities preserve context and
   correction strongly enough in real use?
10. How can humans detect when a favorable Outcome is contaminating Decision
    evaluation?
11. What Evidence demonstrates that Learning improves a later Decision or
    Outcome rather than merely being retrieved?
12. Does real use reveal a failure of existing semantic composition?

These questions create no backlog or authorized validation.

## 19. Additional Validation Questions

The following questions may be relevant to future separately authorized
validation. They do not define or execute validation.

### Outcome Observation

- Is the intended condition fixed and attributable?
- Is the post-Change operational condition observed from an applicable source?
- Are horizon, exposure, measurement meaning, corrections, and missing Evidence
  explicit?
- Are adverse, delayed, displaced, null, and balancing effects visible?

### Interpretation

- Is Interpretation traceable to Outcome Evidence and Decision intent?
- Are alternative explanations and context changes preserved?
- Is Actual Change and handoff fidelity known?
- Does the Interpretation avoid success/failure and causal overclaim?

### Attribution

- What comparison and mechanism support contribution?
- Which external factors and concurrent Changes matter?
- Is the strongest supported conclusion association, contribution,
  insufficient attribution, or indeterminate?

### Learning

- Does the learning statement cite Decision, Actual Change, Outcome, context,
  uncertainty, and attribution limits?
- Can it be corrected or rejected later?
- Does later use require new human judgment?
- Is Learning kept distinct from success, policy, and authority?
- Is there evidence that later judgment or Outcome improved?

### Human and AI authority

- Who owns Outcome verification and Learning judgment?
- Did AI remain assistive and evidence-citing?
- Was any generated explanation treated as Fact, attribution, or authority?

These questions may refine future inquiry but create no validation artifact,
criterion, method, readiness class, or task.

## 20. Research Findings

### Finding 1 — Observation, Interpretation, Attribution, and Learning differ

Each answers a different question and requires a different evidence claim. None
should be inferred automatically from the prior one.

### Finding 2 — Outcome is Decision-relative but not Decision certification

Outcome verification compares observed condition with intent. It does not prove
the Decision was correct in every respect.

### Finding 3 — Positive and negative Outcome do not determine Decision quality

Decision quality must be assessed from Decision-time Evidence, reasoning,
authority, alternatives, and uncertainty as well as later consequence.

### Finding 4 — Learning may arise from every Outcome condition

Desired, undesired, mixed, null, unexpected, and indeterminate Outcomes may all
support bounded Learning, including the conclusion that no causal learning is
supported.

### Finding 5 — Learning cannot declare success or justify harm

The existence of a lesson does not transform consequence, validate the Decision,
or authorize future Change.

### Finding 6 — External factors and handoff fidelity remain material

Context change, unexpected events, incomplete Actual Change, and attribution
uncertainty bound both Outcome Interpretation and Learning.

### Finding 7 — AI cannot own verification or Learning authority

AI may assist evidence organization and comparison but cannot declare Outcome,
attribution, Decision correctness, success, or authoritative lesson.

### Finding 8 — Existing semantic kinds appear conceptually sufficient

Observation, Evidence, Interpretation, Decision, Actual Change, Outcome,
Learning, Memory, and Knowledge can compose the transition without new objects
or frameworks.

### Finding 9 — Real-world product adequacy remains unproven

Outcome Evidence coverage, comparison, attribution, bias control, learning
lineage, correction, and later value remain open evidence questions.

## 21. Research Conclusion

Outcome-to-Learning is best understood as a chain of distinct, human-reviewed,
evidence-bearing propositions:

```text
Operational condition observed
  ≠ Outcome interpreted
  ≠ Outcome attributed
  ≠ Learning justified
  ≠ Future Decision authorized
```

Learning is valid only to the extent that it preserves the Outcome Evidence,
Decision and Actual Change context, attribution limits, uncertainty, adverse and
contrary material, and conditions for correction. A desired Outcome does not
certify the Decision; an undesired Outcome does not condemn it; an indeterminate
Outcome may still teach what cannot be claimed.

Existing Management OS semantic kinds appear conceptually sufficient. The open
questions concern real source Evidence, verification ownership, comparison,
attribution, bias, correction, transfer, and whether Learning improves later
human judgment and operational consequence.
